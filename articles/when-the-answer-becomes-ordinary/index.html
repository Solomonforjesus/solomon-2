When the Answer Becomes Ordinary

Faith, Gratitude, and Everyday Life · November 2, 2026

Scientific Progress, Human Forgetfulness, and Our Continuing Need for God

A family gathers beside a hospital bed. The physician explains the treatment, then pauses. There are reasons to hope, but no guarantees.

The family prays. Please, God. Let this person live.

Treatment begins. Days pass. A number improves, then another. Finally, someone opens the door with the news they have scarcely dared imagine: their loved one is going home.

There are tears, embraces, and grateful prayers.

Years later, the same patient stands in the kitchen, irritated by a late appointment and a dripping faucet. Breakfast needs clearing. The phone rings.

Life has become ordinary again.

That was what everyone prayed for.

Yet the gratitude that once filled the hospital room can disappear while the answer continues. The person still breathes, works, complains, makes plans. The gift remains; the memory fades.

Across generations, the same thing happens on a larger scale. A treatment once greeted with astonishment becomes routine. Children grow up protected from dangers their grandparents feared. They inherit the benefit without inheriting the memory.

Can we receive an answer to prayer so thoroughly that we forget we ever needed to pray?

When success changes the story

We should want children spared their grandparents’ suffering. We should welcome effective treatments, safer homes, and discoveries that give families more years together.

But success can change the story we tell about ourselves.

Long before modern medicine, Moses warned Israel about this danger. In Deuteronomy 8, he pictured people eating well, settling into good houses, and watching their wealth increase. Amid that welcome prosperity, they would be tempted to say, “My power and the might of my hand have gained me this wealth” (Deuteronomy 8:17, NKJV).

Their hands would indeed have worked. Their accomplishments would be real. The mistake would come when they treated their own effort as the whole explanation.

Moses called them to remember Who had given them the ability to accomplish anything.

These words belong to Israel’s covenant history. They do not promise wealth to every faithful Christian. They do, however, expose a temptation we recognize: the easier life becomes, the more readily we mistake comfort for independence.

A surgeon’s judgment matters. So do the engineer’s calculations and the researcher’s years of patient work. Gratitude to God gives us every reason to honor them. Each has used abilities whose existence reaches beyond personal achievement.

Scientific progress can change how urgently we feel our dependence on God. That change in feeling does not establish that we need Him less.

Follow the bread

Set a loaf of bread on the table and trace its journey backward.

Someone stocked the shelf. Someone drove the truck, baked the dough, milled the grain, and harvested the field. Machinery, commerce, weather, soil, and human judgment all contributed.

Every stage can be investigated. Every worker deserves acknowledgment.

The Christian sees that entire journey taking place within a creation none of those workers brought into existence.

We paid for the loaf. The transaction is complete. But paying for bread does not make us the authors of the world that made bread possible.

Colossians 1:16–17 presents Christ as the One through Whom creation came to be and in Whom it holds together. Within that understanding, discovering how something works means learning about the world He sustains.

In Quantum Supremacy, Michio Kaku imagines extraordinary advances in medicine, energy, and other fields. His vision raises a question that reaches beyond the machinery: if even some of our greatest ambitions succeed, what sort of people will we become?

A researcher bends over an experiment today. Years later, someone she will never meet may live because she persisted. A teacher encourages a child who eventually helps preserve another child’s life.

Such work deserves our enthusiasm. Christ’s command to love our neighbors gives practical significance to efforts that relieve their suffering.

Within a Christian understanding of providence, God can work through those efforts. We need not claim to know how every discovery relates to every prayer. We can conduct the experiment carefully, report its results truthfully, and use what we learn to serve.

Then, when the work succeeds, we face another responsibility.

We must remember to return.

The one who turned around

Ten men appealed to Jesus for mercy. He told them to show themselves to the priests. As they went, they were cleansed.

One turned back.

Luke describes him glorifying God and falling at Jesus’ feet in thanksgiving. He was a Samaritan. Jesus asked about the other nine (Luke 17:11–19).

We are not told everything about their motives or their later lives. We are shown something searching enough: all ten received healing; one returned to give thanks.

The need had been met. He recognized that his response was still unfinished.

Those of us who pray should pause here.

A crisis gives prayer urgency. We know what we want, and we ask earnestly. Once the danger passes, the demands of ordinary life reclaim our attention. God can drift toward the margins of the very life we begged Him to preserve.

This rarely requires a deliberate rejection. We simply become busy again.

Religious familiarity offers no protection. We may speak fluently about providence while treating another peaceful day as something owed to us.

What do we want from Christ when nothing is going wrong?

Can we come to Him with no emergency to report? Can we give Him our attention because we love Him?

Before answering, we must walk back into the hospital.

There is another room.

The room where someone dies

Another family prays just as earnestly. The physicians work just as hard.

The treatment fails.

Someone goes home with an empty seat beside them.

Any account of answered prayer that leaves this family outside the argument is incomplete. Recovery cannot measure the strength of someone’s faith. Death cannot establish that God loved someone less.

We do not know enough to explain why one family receives more years and another begins grieving.

Jesus wept at Lazarus’s tomb. Paul repeatedly asked for an affliction to be removed and received grace to endure it. Neither account allows us to make favorable circumstances the foundation of Christian confidence.

Gratitude must leave room for lament. A grieving person may appreciate every act of care and still ache over what medicine could not repair. We have no right to invent a purpose for that person’s pain or demand that the loss itself be called a blessing.

Our hope rests in Christ’s faithfulness and resurrection. It must reach the family whose loved one never comes home.

That hope gives us reason to pursue healing wholeheartedly. Every additional year can matter enormously. Yet healing cannot bear the weight of salvation, and those who remain ill possess undiminished worth.

A neighbor’s claim upon our love does not depend on recovery.

What will we do with what we have?

Suppose tomorrow brings a discovery that makes a previously untreatable illness treatable.

The discovery creates an opportunity. It also brings work: testing, manufacturing, training, delivery, and difficult decisions about resources.

Compassion must attend to those realities. Promising help we cannot provide serves no one.

But yesterday’s inability should not become tomorrow’s automatic excuse. As our abilities grow, we must examine what we can now do for others.

First Timothy 6:17–19 joins the enjoyment of God’s provision with humility and generosity. We may receive good things gladly and use them to sustain our households. We are also called to become generous.

Gratitude gains credibility when it changes what we do with our advantages.

Those advantages should make us careful in judging people who have less. Personal effort matters, but no one chooses every ability, opportunity, or institution upon which a life depends. Success is an incomplete measure of merit and a poor measure of human worth.

Greater technological power brings a related temptation: to assess people chiefly by what can be repaired or how much usefulness can be restored.

The person in the bed was worthy of love before the treatment began.

That remains true when every treatment has been exhausted.

Teach the children what they did not see

A child drinks clean water, receives medicine, and goes outside to play. The child has no memory of the dangers these ordinary benefits have prevented.

That is a mercy.

We should never wish hardship upon children merely to make them appreciative. They need truthful teaching, patient examples, and opportunities to serve.

In Joshua 4, memorial stones prompted questions. Children who had not witnessed God’s deliverance could ask what the stones meant and hear the account.

Our own remembrance also needs a deliberate form.

Tell children about the people who helped your family. Explain the work behind conveniences they take for granted. Honor the researchers, laborers, teachers, and caregivers whose service reaches into their lives.

Let them hear you thank God when there is no crisis.

Then give remembrance something to do. Involve them in caring for a neighbor, maintaining what others built, or giving something they could have kept.

An inherited blessing needs people willing to sustain it. Gratitude reaches backward to acknowledge what was received and forward to accept responsibility.

For Christians, remembrance has its indispensable center at the Lord’s Table. We remember Christ’s death and receive again the reminder that our salvation rests upon a work we did not accomplish.

However capable we become, we remain people who need forgiveness.

More years—and what then?

Imagine medicine giving us longer, healthier lives.

A father sees his children grow. A husband cares for his wife. An estranged friend has time to seek forgiveness.

These possibilities are precious. The certainty of death does not diminish the value of time.

But more time leaves us with a question: how will we live?

Additional years may deepen generosity or harden selfishness. Better health does not produce repentance. An extended lifespan does not reconcile us to God.

Christian hope reaches beyond the postponement of death. In 1 Corinthians 15, Paul locates victory over death in Jesus Christ and the resurrection. In 1 Thessalonians 4:13–18, he comforts grieving believers with the promise of being gathered to the Lord.

Unless we live to see Christ come for His Church, we, too, will die. No technological promise gives us grounds to dismiss that reality.

Even now, in health and comfort, we need Him. Christ died for our sins and rose again. He calls us to repentance and faith while our plans are still proceeding smoothly.

We need not wait for another hospital room to respond.

Return instead to that kitchen.

Breakfast is on the table. The faucet still drips. An ordinary task awaits, and there is no reason for alarm.

The absence of alarm is part of what the family once prayed for.

Gratitude need not recreate the fear of those days. It can be quiet and active: a prayer before the day begins, patience with a spouse, help offered to a neighbor, honest work performed with care.

Another day has been entrusted to us.

Let us continue to seek cures. Let us honor those whose labor relieves suffering and give Jesus Christ glory for the life and gifts that make such service possible.

And when the answer becomes ordinary, let us remember to return.

Christian maturity includes a gratitude that no longer needs a crisis to awaken it.
